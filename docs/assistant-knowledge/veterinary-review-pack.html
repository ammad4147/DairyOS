Worms and parasite control

dairy.disease.parasites · TOPIC · safety VET_ONLY · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: How often should I deworm my cows?; worm control in cattle; do all animals need deworming

Summary served: Routine blanket deworming of every animal is discouraged because it breeds dewormer-resistant worms. Work with the veterinarian to target the animals most at risk (mainly young stock under about 16 months, especially on pasture) and use faecal egg counts to decide and to check that the product still works.

  1. A faecal egg count reduction test compares worm eggs before and after treatment; at least a 95 percent reduction means the dewormer is working.
  2. Under-dosing, often from guessing body weight, drives resistance; weigh animals and follow the product label and veterinarian.
  3. Leaving some of the best-performing animals untreated and resting pastures for 4 to 5 weeks help slow resistance.
  4. Older cattle usually develop tolerance and handle infections better than young stock.
  5. Dewormer purchases are recorded in DairyOS Finance under OPEX (Dewormers & Parasiticides).
Veterinary escalation wording: Choice of dewormer, dose and timing should be set with your veterinarian, including any milk or meat withdrawal.

Summary served: There is no universal schedule; the right programme depends on the diseases present in your area, the animals' ages and pregnancy status, product authorisation and your veterinarian's plan. Recognising dehydration in a calf

dairy.calf.dehydration · TRIAGE · safety TRIAGE · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: How do I know if a calf is dehydrated?; skin tent test calf

Summary served: Judge dehydration from how sunken the eyes are, how long a skin pinch stays tented, the calf's demeanour and whether it can stand and suck. Under about 8 percent dehydration a standing calf can usually be given oral electrolytes; at 8 percent or more it needs intravenous fluids from a veterinarian.

  1. Oklahoma State University's guide describes 6 to 8 percent dehydration as a depressed calf with 2 to 4 mm eye recession and a skin tent lasting 1 to 3 seconds.
  2. At 8 to 10 percent the eyes are recessed 4 to 6 mm and the skin tent lasts 2 to 5 seconds.
  3. At 10 to 12 percent the calf may be comatose, with 6 to 8 mm eye recession and a skin tent of 5 to 10 seconds.
  4. Skin tenting is checked by gently pinching the skin below the eye and watching whether the fold persists.
  5. Calves in sternal or lateral recumbency respond poorly to oral fluids and need intravenous therapy.
Veterinary escalation wording: A calf that is down, not sucking or visibly sunken-eyed needs a veterinarian immediately.

Navel care in newborn calves

Operators ask: How should I treat the calf's navel?; navel dipping calf; swollen navel calf

Summary served: Dip the navel immediately after birth (7 percent tincture of iodine is the usual standard; 2 percent chlorhexidine is an alternative) and keep the calf on clean, dry bedding. A swollen, wet, smelly or painful navel after the first day needs veterinary attention.

  1. Signs of navel infection include swelling, foul discharge, wetness lasting beyond the first day, hardness, tenderness, fever and poor appetite.
  2. Palpating the navel at 3 to 5 days of age helps catch infection early.
  3. By one week of age the umbilical stump should be about thumb-sized.
  4. University of Wisconsin reports that 15 to 20 percent of US dairy calves develop umbilical infections.

Calf pneumonia warning signs

dairy.calf.pneumonia · TRIAGE · safety TRIAGE · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: How do I know if a calf has pneumonia?; calf coughing and nose running; calf breathing fast

Summary served: Watch for cough, nasal or eye discharge, droopy ears or head tilt, fast or laboured breathing and fever. A simple scoring system treats a calf as a respiratory case when its signs add up to 5 or more points.

  1. In the simplified scoring system, cough scores 2, eye discharge 2, fever above 39.2 C (102.6 F) 2, abnormal breathing 2, nasal discharge 4 and ear droop or head tilt 5.
  2. Respiratory disease is the single greatest cause of heifer deaths after weaning in US surveys.
  3. Risk factors include poor colostrum (passive immunity) transfer, inadequate ventilation, high humidity, rapid weather changes, crowding and mixing age groups.
  4. Prevention relies on good colostrum, well-ventilated housing separate from adults, avoiding crowding and age mixing, all-in/all-out groups, good nutrition and a veterinarian-designed vaccination plan.
Veterinary escalation wording: Laboured or open-mouth breathing, a calf off feed, or several calves coughing needs prompt veterinary assessment; antimicrobial choice is a veterinary decision.

What causes calf scours

dairy.calf.scours-causes · TOPIC · safety TRIAGE · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: What causes diarrhea in calves?; calf scours causes; why do calves get loose motion

Summary served: Calf scours is not one disease. Common infectious causes differ by age; E. coli in the first days, rotavirus and coronavirus at about 5 to 15 days, Cryptosporidium in the first 2 weeks, and Salmonella from 2 to 12 weeks, often worsened by poor colostrum and hygiene.

  1. Enterotoxigenic E. coli affects calves under 3 to 5 days old and causes profuse watery diarrhoea.
  2. Rotavirus is the most common viral cause and, like coronavirus, usually affects calves 5 to 15 days old.
  3. Cryptosporidium parvum is among the most common infectious causes in the first 2 weeks of life.
  4. Salmonella affects calves about 2 to 12 weeks old and can spread to the bloodstream.
  5. The cause cannot be identified from appearance alone; laboratory testing guided by the veterinarian identifies it.
Veterinary escalation wording: Several scouring calves, blood in the manure, or a weak calf needs the veterinarian.

Bovine viral diarrhoea (BVD)

dairy.disease.bvd · TRIAGE · safety TRIAGE · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: What is BVD?; persistently infected calf BVD

Summary served: BVD is a viral disease of cattle whose main effect is suppressing the immune system. Calves infected in the womb during the first 4 months of pregnancy can be born persistently infected (PI); they carry and shed the virus for life and are the main source of spread.

  1. Signs in infected cattle can include fever, dullness, lower milk, diarrhoea, respiratory disease and abortion.
  2. PI calves often grow poorly and many die before 2 years of age, sometimes from severe mucosal disease with mouth lesions.
  3. PI animals shed large amounts of virus and infect herd-mates; the virus can also spread through insects, equipment and semen.
  4. Control means testing replacements and bulls, removing PI animals, biosecurity, and vaccination planned with the veterinarian.
Veterinary escalation wording: Unexplained abortions, poor-doing calves or several animals with diarrhoea or breathing problems need veterinary testing.
Sources: Merck Veterinary Manual: Bovine Viral Diarrhea and Mucosal Disease Complex (2026-01)

Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD)

dairy.disease.fmd · TRIAGE · safety TRIAGE · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: What are the signs of foot and mouth disease?; blisters in mouth cow drooling

Summary served: FMD is a highly contagious viral disease of cloven-hoofed animals causing fever, blisters on the tongue, lips, mouth, teats and between the claws, drooling, lameness and a sharp fall in milk. It is a WOAH-listed disease that must be reported.

  1. Young animals have higher mortality (20 percent or more) than adults (1 to 5 percent).
  2. It spreads through infected animals' secretions, contaminated feed, water, equipment and vehicles.
  3. Control relies on biosecurity, movement restrictions and vaccination with inactivated vaccines.
  4. FMD is not readily transmissible to humans.

Mastitis warning signs

dairy.mastitis.signs · TRIAGE · safety TRIAGE · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: What are common mastitis warning signs?; how to know cow has mastitis; clots in milk; udder swollen hot

Summary served: Clinical mastitis shows as abnormal milk (clots, flakes, watery or discoloured milk) with or without a swollen, hot or painful quarter. Severe cases add fever, loss of appetite, dullness or shock.

  1. The Merck Veterinary Manual grades mild and moderate cases as local signs only, and severe cases as including systemic illness such as fever, anorexia or shock.
  2. Subclinical mastitis has no visible signs and is found only by testing milk, for example with the California Mastitis Test or somatic cell count.
  3. Forestripping three or four streams from each teat before milking helps find abnormal milk early.
  4. Milk from a cow with abnormal milk or under treatment must be kept out of the saleable milk.
Veterinary escalation wording: A cow with abnormal milk plus fever, dullness, cold ears, not eating or a very swollen quarter may have severe (toxic) mastitis and needs a veterinarian urgently. Tube or injection choice and dose are veterinary decisions.

Bloat

Operators ask: Cow belly swollen on left side, what to do?; bloat in cows

Summary served: Bloat is gas trapped in the rumen, causing sudden swelling of the left flank, distress, laboured breathing and death within hours if not relieved. Frothy bloat follows lush legume pasture or grain; free-gas bloat often comes from an obstruction such as choke.

  1. The Merck Veterinary Manual notes death commonly occurs 3 to 4 hours after signs begin.
  2. Introduce legumes such as lucerne or berseem gradually, feed hay before turning onto legume pasture, and avoid finely ground grain.
Veterinary escalation wording: Bloat is an emergency. Call the veterinarian immediately, keep the animal standing and walking gently, and do not attempt stomach puncture unless trained and directed.

dairy.nutrition.sara · TOPIC · safety TRIAGE · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: What is acidosis in cows?; loose manure low milk fat

Summary served: Subacute ruminal acidosis is repeated periods of low rumen pH (about 5.6 to 5.2) from too much rapidly fermentable carbohydrate, too little effective fibre, sorting or slug feeding. It shows at herd level as variable intake, lower milk and milk fat, loose manure and lameness.

  1. It is diagnosed at herd level, for example by measuring rumen pH in at-risk cows.
  2. Prevention relies on balanced fibre and carbohydrate, good bunk management to prevent sorting and slug feeding, and adequate buffering.
  3. Sudden access to large amounts of grain (grain overload) is an emergency.

Summary served: Once the water bag or feet appear, a normal calving is usually finished within about 70 minutes, with visible progress every 15 to 20 minutes. Examine the cow if progress stops, if only one leg or an abnormal presentation is seen, or if straining continues without progress.

Summary served: Milk fever is low blood calcium around calving. It usually appears within 48 hours of calving and progresses from excitability and trembling, to lying on the chest with cold ears and a dull expression, to lying flat and unconscious.

  1. The Merck Veterinary Manual describes stage 2 as sternal recumbency with dullness, poor appetite, dry muzzle, low body temperature, cold extremities and sometimes an S-shaped neck.

A good milking routine

Operators ask: What is the correct milking routine?; how to prepare cow for milking; pre dip post dip steps

Summary served: Forestrip three or four streams per teat, pre-dip and leave for about 30 seconds, wipe and dry each teat with an individual towel, attach the unit within 60 to 120 seconds of starting preparation, turn off vacuum before removal, then post-dip the whole teat.

  1. These steps are the National Mastitis Council's recommended milking procedures.
  2. Attaching within 60 to 120 seconds of starting preparation matches the cow's milk let-down.
  3. Never pour used dip back into the original container.
  4. Gloves should be worn and disinfected or changed frequently.

Body condition scoring

dairy.nutrition.bcs · TOPIC · safety EDUCATIONAL · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: What body condition score should a cow have?; bcs at calving; thin cow body condition

Summary served: Body condition is scored 1 (very thin) to 5 (very fat) by feeling the tailhead, pins, hooks and short ribs. Penn State targets about 3.5 at calving and dry-off, about 3.0 in early lactation and 2.75 at peak milk, with no more than 0.5 to 1.0 point lost after calving.

  1. Over-conditioned cows face more metabolic disease, calving difficulty and fertility problems.
  2. Thin cows show weaker heats, lower conception and too little reserve for milk.
  3. DairyOS records BCS and the measurement method under Record Body Development in the Passport.

Summary served: A TMR mixes forages, concentrates and minerals so every bite is similar. The key is consistency; weigh ingredients accurately, check forage dry matter regularly, follow the mixer's mixing time, and avoid over- or under-mixing.

  1. Penn State Extension advises checking feed dry matter weekly because moisture changes alter what cows actually receive.

Water for dairy cows

dairy.nutrition.water · TOPIC · safety EDUCATIONAL · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: How much water does a dairy cow need?; water trough space cows

Summary served: Water is the most important nutrient; a high-producing cow may drink up to about 50 gallons (roughly 190 L) a day, and more in hot weather. Give easy access, especially right after milking, with enough trough space and clean water.

  1. A lactating cow drinks 30 to 60 percent of her daily water shortly after milking.
  2. Water intake rises by about 0.9 pounds for each pound of milk produced and increases with temperature.
  3. Heat can raise consumption by 50 to 100 percent.
  4. Test water for total dissolved solids, nitrates, sulphates and bacteria.
  5. Provide at least about 3 inches of accessible trough space per cow, per University of Maryland Extension.
Sources: South Dakota State University Extension: Water Importance in Lactating Dairy Cows (2021-10-15); University of Maryland Extension: Short- and Long-term Effects of Heat Stress in Dairy Cattle (2021-05-07)

When to inseminate after heat is seen (AM-PM rule)

dairy.repro.ai-timing · TOPIC · safety EDUCATIONAL · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: When should I inseminate a cow after heat?; am pm rule; best time for AI; sexed semen timing

Summary served: Under the AM-PM rule, a cow seen in standing heat in the morning is inseminated that evening, and one seen in the evening is inseminated the next morning. Conventional semen is best used about 12 to 18 hours after standing heat begins; sexed semen a little later, about 18 to 24 hours.

  1. Ovulation occurs about 30 hours after the start of standing heat, which is why insemination is timed after heat onset.
  2. Sexed semen has a shorter fertile life, so timing is more critical.
  3. Follow the AI technician's and semen supplier's handling instructions for thawing and handling straws.

Summary served: A cow that is not pregnant comes into heat about every 21 days (range roughly 17 to 24 days). Standing heat lasts on average about 18 hours, with a range of about 8 to 24 hours.

Summary served: Gestation averages about 279 days in Holstein-Friesian cows, with normal variation of roughly 260 to 300 days; breed matters most, and zebu cattle average about 296 days. DairyOS uses a fixed 283 days from insemination as its planning rule for expected calving.

  1. The Merck Veterinary Manual states a mean of 279 days for Holstein-Friesian cattle, with a normal range of about 260 to 300 days.

Summary served: The key sign is standing to be mounted by another cow or bull. Secondary signs include mounting others, restlessness, bellowing, a swollen vulva and clear stringy mucus. Watch the herd at least twice a day, morning and evening.

Pregnancy diagnosis methods and timing

dairy.repro.pregnancy-diagnosis · TOPIC · safety EDUCATIONAL · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: How early can a cow be pregnancy checked?; pd by ultrasound how many days; blood pregnancy test cows

Summary served: A veterinarian can diagnose pregnancy by ultrasound or rectal palpation, typically around 35 days after insemination. Blood or milk pregnancy-associated glycoprotein (PAG) tests work from about 28 days. Not coming back into heat is only a rough early indicator.

  1. NADIS lists non-return to heat at 18 to 24 days as a poor indicator, with about 30 percent false positives at 21 days.
  2. Milk progesterone at 24 days is over 95 percent accurate for identifying non-pregnant cows but gives 20 to 25 percent false positives for pregnancy.
  3. Experienced ultrasound or rectal examination is over 95 percent accurate, with about 35 days as the standard timing.
  4. Some early pregnancies are lost, so a positive early diagnosis may need rechecking.
  5. Finding open cows early lets them be re-bred sooner.
Sources: NADIS Animal Health Skills: Fertility in Dairy Herds Part 5: Pregnancy Diagnosis (2017)

The dry period and drying off

dairy.transition.dry-period · TOPIC · safety EDUCATIONAL · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: How long should the dry period be?; what is a dry cow; how to dry off a cow

Summary served: A dry cow is a pregnant cow that has stopped being milked before her next calving. A dry period of about 45 to 65 days (commonly around 60) lets the udder regenerate; cows with no dry period produce much less in the next lactation.

  1. Alabama Extension reports that cows with no dry period produce only about 75 percent as much milk as cows given a 50 to 60 day break.
  2. Both much longer and much shorter dry periods tend to reduce milk in the next lactation.
  3. Abrupt dry-off is common when yield is already low; higher-yielding cows may be dried off gradually by reducing milking frequency, following the veterinarian's advice.
  4. Dry cow therapy and internal teat sealants protect the udder during the dry period; which products to use is a veterinary decision.
  5. Dry-off is recorded in DairyOS by changing the cow's life stage to Dry.
Sources: Alabama Cooperative Extension System: Managing and Caring for the Dry Cow (2021-05); Merck Veterinary Manual: Mastitis in Cattle (2025-04)

The transition period

dairy.transition.overview · TOPIC · safety EDUCATIONAL · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: What is the transition period in dairy cows?; why are fresh cows high risk

Summary served: The transition period is the time around calving, traditionally three weeks before to three weeks after, now often viewed as 60 days before to 30 days after. Most metabolic and infectious disease of dairy cows happens in this window.

  1. University of Minnesota Extension notes that more than 35 percent of dairy cows have at least one clinical disease event in the first 90 days in milk.
  2. Key transition diseases are milk fever (hypocalcaemia), ketosis, retained placenta, metritis, displaced abomasum and mastitis.
  3. Fresh cows should be observed daily during the first two weeks of lactation.
  4. Feed intake falls before calving and must recover quickly afterwards; low intake drives ketosis and displaced abomasum risk.
Sources: University of Minnesota Extension: Managing and monitoring the health of transition dairy cows (2026); Merck Veterinary Manual: Hyperketonemia in Cattle (2024-07)

Cow comfort and lying time

dairy.welfare.cow-comfort · TOPIC · safety EDUCATIONAL · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: How many hours should cows lie down?; cow comfort tips; what bedding is best for cows

Summary served: Dairy cows need about 10 to 14 hours of lying down each day. Clean, dry, soft bedding, enough stalls, avoiding overcrowding and good ventilation protect milk, health and fertility.

  1. University of Kentucky notes cows need at least 40 hours to recover after being deprived of 2 to 4 hours of rest.
  2. Deep sand bedding is regarded as the gold standard for reducing hock injuries and lameness; mattresses need bedding on top.
  3. Stocking close to 100 percent (one stall per cow) is optimal, and fresh cows should never be crowded.
  4. Ventilation needs rise from at least 4 air changes per hour in winter to 40 to 60 per hour in hot weather.
  5. Long standing times are linked to lameness.
Sources: University of Kentucky Department of Animal and Food Sciences: Improving and Maintaining Cow Comfort; University of Maryland Extension: Short- and Long-term Effects of Heat Stress in Dairy Cattle (2021-05-07)

Low-stress cattle handling

dairy.welfare.handling · TOPIC · safety EDUCATIONAL · current status SOURCE_CURATED

Operators ask: How should cows be handled calmly?; how to move cows without stress

Summary served: Move cows slowly and calmly with a low voice, work from where they can see you, and use the flight zone and the point of balance at the shoulder instead of shouting, hitting or tail twisting.

  1. Standing behind the shoulder moves a cow forward; standing in front of it slows or stops her.
  2. Cows see about 330 degrees around them but not directly behind, so approach from the front or side.
  3. Sudden or high-pitched noise, hitting, slapping and tail twisting cause fear and stress.
  4. Calm handling reduces injuries to animals and people and helps milk let-down.
Sources: University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension: The Science of Cow Handling Made Practical (2026-08)
